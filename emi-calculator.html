What this calculator does

This tool works out the fixed monthly payment on any loan — a personal loan, auto loan, mortgage, or (in India) an EMI — based on three inputs: how much you're borrowing, the annual interest rate, and how long you'll take to repay it. It then breaks that payment down into how much is principal versus interest, and lets you test what happens if you pay a bit extra each month.

How the calculation works

Loans with a fixed monthly payment use amortization: the lender calculates one payment amount that, if paid every month for the full term, exactly pays off both the loan and all the interest that accrues along the way. Because interest is charged on the remaining balance, and that balance is highest at the very start, early payments are mostly interest. As the balance shrinks, more of each payment goes toward principal — even though the payment amount itself never changes.

The formula

The standard formula is:

Payment = P × r × (1 + r)n / [(1 + r)n − 1]
where P = loan amount, r = monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and n = number of monthly payments.

Worked example

Say you borrow $25,000 at 6.5% annual interest over 5 years (60 months). The monthly rate is 6.5% ÷ 12 = 0.542%. Plugging that into the formula gives a fixed monthly payment of roughly $489. Over the full 5 years, you'd repay about $29,340 in total — meaning around $4,340 of that is interest, not principal.

Practical use cases

  • Comparing loan offers from different lenders before you apply
  • Checking whether a shorter term with a higher payment saves meaningfully on interest
  • Deciding whether making extra payments is worth it once you have spare cash
  • Getting a rough mortgage estimate before speaking with a lender

Advantages of using a payment calculator first

Lenders quote interest rates, but rarely lead with the total dollar cost of interest over the full term — that number can be more persuasive than the rate itself. Running the numbers yourself before applying means you're negotiating from a position of knowing exactly what a rate difference of even half a percent costs you in real terms over the life of the loan.

Limitations to keep in mind

This calculator assumes a fixed interest rate for the full term and doesn't account for origination fees, closing costs, prepayment penalties, or variable-rate adjustments — all of which can change your real cost. For mortgages specifically, property tax and insurance escrow are not included, so your actual monthly outgoing will likely be higher than the payment figure shown here.

Common mistakes people make

  • Focusing only on the monthly payment and ignoring total interest paid over the full term
  • Assuming a lower monthly payment always means a cheaper loan — a longer term often costs more overall even at a lower payment
  • Not checking whether extra payments actually reduce principal on their specific loan (some loans apply extra payments differently)
  • Forgetting that a lower rate with high fees can cost more than a slightly higher rate with none

Frequently asked questions

How is a loan payment calculated?

It's calculated using the loan amount, the interest rate, and the loan term, spread evenly across every monthly payment via the amortization formula above.

Does paying extra each month actually help?

Yes — extra payments reduce your principal directly, which lowers the interest charged on every future payment and can shorten your loan term.

Why does most of my early payment go to interest?

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is highest early in the loan. As the balance falls, more of each payment shifts toward principal.

What's the difference between the rate and the true cost of a loan?

The rate is only one input. The true cost includes total interest paid across the full term, plus any fees — which is why the total-interest figure matters as much as the rate.

Can this calculator be used for a mortgage?

The core math applies, so it gives a usable payment estimate — though real mortgages typically add property tax and insurance costs not modeled here.

A few practical tips

  • Even a small extra monthly payment early in the loan saves more interest than the same amount paid later
  • Always compare the total interest figure, not just the monthly payment, when weighing two loan offers
  • Check whether your lender charges a prepayment penalty before planning to pay extra

Conclusion

A loan's monthly payment only tells part of the story — the total interest over the full term is often the number that actually determines whether a loan is a good deal. Use the calculator above with a few different rates and terms side by side before committing to any offer.
