What this calculator does

This tool estimates your take-home pay by applying progressive national income tax bands to your gross annual salary. It shows both the total tax and, in the bracket breakdown, exactly how much of your income falls into each tax band and what's owed at each rate.

How the calculation works

Progressive tax systems don't apply one flat rate to your entire salary. Instead, your income is split into brackets, and each bracket is taxed at its own rate — only the portion of income that falls within a bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. This is why your effective (average) tax rate is always lower than your top bracket's rate.

Worked example

On a $60,000 gross salary in the US, the first portion is taxed at 10%, the next portion at 12%, and so on up through whichever bracket your income reaches — resulting in a total estimated tax and an effective rate that's noticeably lower than the top bracket touched.

Practical use cases

  • Comparing take-home pay across two job offers with different gross salaries
  • Understanding how a raise affects your net pay, not just your gross figure
  • Getting a quick estimate before a detailed payroll calculation
  • Seeing how much of a salary increase is actually kept versus taxed away

Advantages of checking this before negotiating

A salary offer is often discussed in gross terms, but what actually matters day-to-day is net pay. Knowing your effective tax rate in advance means you can evaluate a raise or offer in terms of what you'll actually receive, not just the headline number.

Limitations to keep in mind

This estimate covers national income tax bands only. It does not include state or local taxes, Social Security, National Insurance, EPF or similar payroll contributions, pension deductions, or personal tax credits and allowances — all of which affect your real paycheck. Treat this as a directional estimate, not a substitute for a payroll calculation or tax advisor.

Common mistakes people make

  • Assuming their top tax bracket applies to their entire salary, rather than just the portion above that threshold
  • Comparing gross salary offers without accounting for how tax brackets differ between two roles or locations
  • Forgetting that payroll deductions beyond income tax (like Social Security or NI) further reduce actual take-home pay
  • Not re-checking the estimate after a raise pushes income into a new bracket

Frequently asked questions

How is take-home pay estimated?

By applying progressive tax bands to gross income, taxing each portion at its bracket's rate, then subtracting the total from gross pay.

Why might my actual paycheck differ from this estimate?

This models national income tax only — state/local tax, Social Security, NI, EPF and personal credits aren't included, so real pay will differ.

Why is my effective tax rate lower than my top bracket?

Only the income above each threshold is taxed at that bracket's rate, so your blended average rate is always below your top bracket.

Does this include Social Security or National Insurance?

No — this covers national income tax bands only; payroll contributions like these would reduce take-home pay further.

A few practical tips

  • Always compare net pay, not gross salary, when evaluating two offers
  • Check whether a raise pushes any portion of your income into a new bracket before assuming the full amount is "extra" take-home
  • Use this as a starting estimate, then confirm exact figures with a payroll calculator or tax professional

Conclusion

The gap between your gross salary and what you actually take home can be larger than expected — and it's driven by exactly which brackets your income touches. Use the breakdown above to see precisely where that gap comes from.
