What this calculator does

This tool projects how a savings or investment balance grows over time, combining a starting amount, a regular monthly contribution, and an expected annual return. It separates the final balance into what you actually put in versus what compound growth added on top — so you can see how much of your future balance is genuinely "free" money from growth.

How the calculation works

Each month, your contribution is added to the current balance, and then the whole balance grows by your monthly return rate (the annual rate divided by 12). Because growth is calculated on the full balance — including money that already grew in previous months — the effect compounds: growth builds on growth, not just on your original contributions.

Worked example

Starting with $1,000 and contributing $200 a month at a 7% expected annual return over 10 years, the balance grows to roughly $35,000. Of that, around $25,000 came directly from your own contributions, and about $10,000 came purely from compound growth.

Practical use cases

  • Estimating how a retirement or investment account could grow over decades
  • Testing whether increasing a monthly contribution meaningfully changes the long-term outcome
  • Comparing a lower starting amount with a higher monthly contribution against the reverse
  • Setting a realistic savings goal timeline for a specific target amount

Advantages of projecting growth early

Compounding rewards time more than it rewards contribution size — starting small early often outperforms starting large late, because early growth has more years to compound on itself. Seeing this visually, rather than just being told it, tends to make the case for starting sooner more convincing.

Limitations to keep in mind

This calculator assumes a constant annual return for the entire period, but real markets and interest rates fluctuate year to year — some years may be flat or negative even if the long-term average matches your input. It also doesn't account for taxes, account fees, or inflation, all of which reduce real purchasing power of the final balance.

Common mistakes people make

  • Assuming a market average return applies evenly every single year rather than averaging out over time
  • Ignoring the difference between nominal growth and inflation-adjusted (real) growth
  • Underestimating how much monthly contributions matter compared to the starting amount
  • Not accounting for fees or taxes that reduce the effective return in a real account

Frequently asked questions

How does compound interest grow savings?

Each period's growth is calculated on the full balance, including previously earned growth, so the balance accelerates the longer it stays invested.

Does contributing monthly make a big difference?

Yes — regular contributions add new principal that also compounds, so consistent smaller amounts can outgrow a single larger deposit made later.

Why does growth accelerate in later years?

Because interest is earned on both contributions and previously earned growth, the balance grows faster each year even at the same contribution level.

What return rate should I assume?

It depends on where the money sits — savings accounts earn far less than diversified investments, so use a rate that matches your actual account type.

A few practical tips

  • Starting a few years earlier often matters more than contributing a bit more per month
  • Use a conservative return estimate rather than an optimistic one when planning long-term goals
  • Revisit the numbers periodically — actual returns will differ from any single assumed rate

Conclusion

The gap between "contributed" and "final balance" in the results above is the real case for starting to save early — it's growth you didn't have to work for. Try a few different time horizons above to see how much that gap widens the longer money stays invested.
